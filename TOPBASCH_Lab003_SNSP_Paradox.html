About Léargas SI — methodological note

Léargas SI (Systems Intelligence) is a domain-specific analytical framework built on the foundation of the PESTLE methodology — refining it for energy system analysis. Where PESTLE applies six generic dimensions, Léargas SI replaces the Economic and Environmental lenses with a domain-calibrated Exergetic lens: evaluating not just energy quantity but thermodynamic quality, grade, and recovery potential.

The six lenses — Technical, Financial, Political, Ecological, Social, Exergetic — retain PESTLE's structural familiarity while enabling analytically precise evaluation of energy infrastructure decisions. This whitepaper is the third published application of Léargas SI v2, following the Ireland Data Centre case (001) and the Hornsea 3 grid connection crisis (002).

Section 1

The Paradox

Ireland is among the windiest countries in Europe — and among the most aggressive in its renewable electricity targets. The Climate Action Plan mandates 80% renewable electricity by 2030, up from approximately 42% in 2023. The physical resource is not the constraint. The system is.

The System Non-Synchronous Penetration (SNSP) limit defines the maximum share of electricity that can be generated by non-synchronous sources — wind, solar, and HVDC interconnector imports — at any moment. When this ceiling is reached, EirGrid must curtail renewable output and maintain conventional synchronous generators online to provide system inertia.

In 2024, 96% of all curtailment in the Republic of Ireland was caused by the minimum conventional generation requirement — not by transmission constraints, not by excess supply, but by the system's own operating rules forcing fossil fuel generators to run.

Source: EirGrid Annual Renewable Energy Constraint and Curtailment Report 2024 [3]

This is not a technology failure. Ireland's wind farms are operational. Its grid is connected. The constraint is institutional: a system operating rule — the SNSP cap — is converting available clean energy into waste while simultaneously paying fossil fuel operators to remain online. The cost of this arrangement is projected to reach €700 million in 2025/26.

Section 2

The Mechanism: Why the Cap Exists and Why It Is Now a Liability

The original rationale

The SNSP limit was designed to protect grid frequency stability. Synchronous generators — gas turbines, large steam units — provide physical inertia: their rotating mass resists frequency deviations. Wind turbines and solar inverters do not share this property. As their share of generation rises, the grid's inherent frequency response degrades, increasing the risk of cascading failure during sudden supply or demand changes.

When the SNSP cap was introduced and progressively raised to 75%, it represented genuine engineering leadership: Ireland became one of the highest-penetration non-synchronous grids in the world. But the architecture supporting that limit — requiring a minimum number of conventional synchronous units online at all times — was designed for a system with far less renewable capacity than Ireland now operates.

Why the architecture is now the problem

EirGrid currently requires a minimum of seven large conventional generators to be online across the island at all times. This minimum generation floor creates a structural floor on fossil fuel dispatch — regardless of renewable availability. When wind output is high and demand is low (typically at night, and increasingly during the day), the system reaches its SNSP ceiling with conventional generators still running at minimum output. Renewables are curtailed to maintain the balance.

The paradox is architectural: the safety mechanism designed to enable high renewable penetration is now the binding constraint on further penetration. Ireland has outgrown the operating envelope its own grid rules were designed for — and the rules have not kept pace.

The Grid-Forming connection

EirGrid's Grid-Forming (GFM) Strategy — published in 2024 — directly addresses this architecture. Grid-forming inverters can provide synthetic inertia, enabling the SNSP limit to be raised toward 95% without requiring conventional synchronous generators as the stability backstop. The strategy is correctly diagnosed. The implementation timeline is the gap: GFM deployment at the scale required to raise the SNSP limit to 95% is a multi-year infrastructure programme, while curtailment costs compound annually.

Section 3

Six-Lens Assessment — Léargas SI v2

LensAnalysis
Technical The SNSP limit at 75% is a technically sound response to synchronous inertia requirements — but it was calibrated for a system with lower renewable penetration. The pathway to 95% SNSP requires three parallel interventions: grid-forming inverter deployment at scale, reduction in minimum conventional generation requirements (trials underway to reduce from 8 to 7 units, targeting further reductions), and expanded battery storage providing fast frequency response as a substitute for synchronous inertia. Each of these is technically proven; the constraint is deployment pace versus curtailment cost accumulation.
Key variable: GFM inverter procurement and commissioning timeline versus annual €700M curtailment cost trajectory.
Financial Curtailment costs in Ireland have escalated from approximately €450M in 2024 to a projected €700M in 2025/26 — a 56% increase in one year. Over a five-year horizon at current trajectory, cumulative curtailment costs exceed €3.5 billion. By contrast, the capital cost of grid-forming inverter deployment and the synchronous condenser programme required to raise SNSP to 95% is estimated in the hundreds of millions — a fraction of the curtailment cost it would avoid. The financial case for accelerated GFM deployment is unambiguous on a net present value basis. The barrier is institutional: capital budgets are annual, while curtailment costs accumulate across decades.
Additionally: Ireland's Capacity Remuneration Mechanism cleared at €150,000/MW for 2028/29 delivery — the system is paying for the capacity it simultaneously curtails.
Political Ireland's Climate Action Plan mandates 80% renewable electricity by 2030. The current curtailment trajectory — reaching 50% of available wind output by 2030 if unaddressed — is arithmetically incompatible with this target. Every percentage point of curtailment reduces the effective contribution of installed renewable capacity, requiring additional capacity to compensate. The political exposure is significant: Ireland risks missing its own legislated climate targets not because it failed to build wind farms, but because it failed to reform the operating rules governing them. The SNSP cap is a regulatory constraint, not a physical one — it can be changed faster than infrastructure can be built.
Ecological Each GWh of curtailed wind energy is replaced by fossil fuel generation from the minimum generation fleet. In 2025, 11.3% of available wind in the Republic was dispatched down — approximately 2.1 TWh across the island. At a gas plant heat rate and standard emissions factor, this represents several hundred thousand tonnes of avoidable CO₂ annually. Curtailment is not a zero-carbon event: it is a substitution event in which clean generation is replaced by fossil generation. The ecological cost of the SNSP cap is therefore double — lost clean energy and additional emissions from the gas plants kept online to enforce it.
IIEA modelling suggests curtailment could reach 50% of wind output by 2030 at current trajectory, effectively halving the decarbonisation value of Ireland's offshore wind programme. [5]
Social Curtailment costs are socialised through electricity bills. The Capacity Remuneration Mechanism — which pays conventional generators to remain available, including during curtailment events — will add over €7 billion to Irish energy bills between 2018 and 2037, with the data centre demand surge potentially extending this burden. Irish household electricity bills have already increased by over €600 per year since 2020. The SNSP cap is not an abstract engineering parameter: it is a cost mechanism that transfers wealth from electricity consumers to fossil fuel operators in exchange for inertia that could increasingly be provided by grid-forming inverters and storage.
Exergetic This is the Léargas SI differentiator. Wind energy at point of curtailment is high-exergy electricity — maximum thermodynamic quality, directly usable without conversion losses. When it is curtailed, its exergetic value is destroyed entirely. The gas generation that replaces it converts chemical exergy (fuel) to electrical exergy at approximately 50% thermal efficiency, destroying the remainder. The net exergetic loss of each curtailment event is therefore: the full electrical exergy of the curtailed wind, plus the exergy destruction of the replacement gas combustion. This is not merely a financial loss — it is a thermodynamic loss that cannot be recovered. Every hour of curtailment under the SNSP cap represents irreversible destruction of high-quality energy that the Irish wind resource took no fuel to produce.
Exergetic framing distinguishes "wasted energy" from "destroyed energy." Curtailment is the latter — it cannot be stored or recovered after the fact.

Section 4

The Regulatory Pathway — Three Levers

The SNSP paradox is not a physical constraint — it is a regulatory one. The tools to address it exist within current institutional mandates. Three levers are available without new legislation:

Lever 1 · EirGrid

Accelerate Grid-Forming inverter procurement and synchronous condenser deployment. Raise SNSP operational limit from 75% to 85% by 2027 as GFM capacity comes online — as already outlined in the GFM Strategy. Each percentage point increase in the SNSP limit expands the renewable absorption window and directly reduces curtailment hours.

Lever 2 · CRU

Reform the Capacity Remuneration Mechanism to apply derating factors to conventional generators during periods of high renewable availability. Reduce the incentive for fossil generators to remain online when wind could meet demand. The 2028 CRM renewal with the European Commission is the natural policy window — the regulatory framework must be redesigned before that deadline.

Lever 3 · EirGrid / SONI

Reduce the minimum conventional generation requirement from 7 units to 5 — trials for the 7-unit floor are already underway as of April 2024. Each unit removed from the minimum generation floor creates additional headroom for renewable penetration during low-demand periods, directly targeting the 96% of curtailment attributable to this requirement.

Lever 4 · Celtic Interconnector

The 700 MW Ireland–France HVDC interconnector (EirGrid project, operational target 2027) provides an export route for curtailed renewable energy during high-wind periods. Excess wind that would otherwise be dispatched down can instead be exported. The interconnector is also a GFM-adjacent tool: HVDC systems with grid-forming capability contribute to synthetic inertia.

Section 5

Scope and Limitations

What this paper claims — and does not claim

✓The annual cost of curtailment under the SNSP cap (€567M actual 2024/25, €700M projected 2025/26) is quantified from verified market sources and represents a structural rather than cyclical cost.
✓96% of curtailment in Ireland in 2024 was attributable to the minimum conventional generation requirement — not to transmission constraints or SNSP itself. This is a regulatory design feature, not a physical limit.
✓The financial case for accelerated GFM deployment is positive on a net present value basis at the curtailment cost trajectory observed. This is a tractable conclusion from publicly available figures.
○This paper does not model the full SEM dispatch mechanism or the precise regulatory structure of curtailment payments. Cost figures are indicative, not tariff-precise.
○GFM deployment timelines and costs are drawn from EirGrid's published strategy — actual procurement and commissioning paths may differ.
○The exergetic analysis is conceptual rather than thermodynamically quantified. A full exergy accounting would require hourly dispatch data and fuel composition data for replaced generation.
Section 6

Recommended Next Step

The financial arithmetic is clear: Ireland is spending approximately €700 million per year to enforce an operating rule that was designed to enable renewable penetration but is now limiting it. The GFM Strategy correctly identifies the solution. The gap is urgency — the strategy is calibrated to a multi-year deployment horizon while curtailment costs accumulate annually.

Three actions within current institutional mandates would begin to close this gap:

— CRU to publish a curtailment cost trajectory forecast alongside the 2028 CRM renewal consultation, making the financial exposure of the status quo explicit in the regulatory record
— EirGrid to publish a binding SNSP escalation schedule linked to GFM commissioning milestones, creating accountability for the reform timeline
— DESNZ and DECC to jointly commission a cross-jurisdictional study on minimum generation floor reduction — the Northern Ireland constraint is distinct from the Republic's and requires coordinated regulatory response

Ireland has built one of the world's most ambitious renewable electricity systems. The SNSP paradox is not a technology failure or a resource failure — it is a rules failure. The operating architecture was designed for a system Ireland has already surpassed. The question is not whether to reform it, but how fast the institutional machinery can move relative to the rate at which curtailment costs compound. On current trajectory, every year of delay costs Ireland approximately €700 million — not in future investment, but in energy already generated and already discarded.
